Why AI Transformation Is a Business Transformation, Not a Technology Project

Making AI work for business starts with changing how work gets done.

By Steve Liliopoulos · Business AI & Digital Transformation

Artificial intelligence seems to be everywhere right now. Every week brings another announcement about a new AI model, an intelligent agent or a tool promising to change the way we work.

For business leaders, there's plenty to be excited about. But there's also a question worth asking:

How does all this technology actually make our business better?

Because giving people access to AI and transforming a business with AI are two very different things.

One introduces new tools. The other changes how work gets done.

And that's where I believe the bigger opportunity sits.

Start with the business problem, not the technology

Think about a customer service team dealing with hundreds of enquiries every day.

Staff are switching between systems, looking for information, following up with other departments and manually updating customer records.

Now imagine giving everyone an AI assistant.

It might help them draft emails, summarise conversations and find information faster. That's useful. It saves time and makes parts of their day easier.

But what happens if customers are still waiting days for an answer?

The same hand-offs are there. Information is still scattered across different systems. And people are still chasing updates from other teams.

We've made individual tasks faster, but we haven't necessarily improved the overall customer experience.

That's the difference between using AI and transforming a business with AI.

The real opportunity is to look at the entire process, understand where things slow down and ask whether there's a better way to deliver the outcome.

Rethinking how work gets done

Most organisations have built their processes over many years.

New systems are introduced. Different teams develop their own ways of working. Spreadsheets fill the gaps between applications. Manual approvals and workarounds become part of everyday business.

Eventually, these processes become so familiar that people stop questioning them.

AI gives us a reason to look at them again.

Consider something as straightforward as a customer placing an order.

That order might involve sales, finance, inventory, logistics and customer service. Each team has an important role, but information doesn't always move smoothly between them.

What if those systems and teams were better connected?

A potential delivery delay could be identified earlier. A customer could receive an update before needing to call. A service representative could see the full order history without searching across several applications.

AI could help identify issues, recommend actions and automate routine steps, while people remain responsible for important decisions and exceptions.

This isn't simply about doing the same work faster.

It's about creating a more connected way of working, where information reaches the right people at the right time.

And that can make a meaningful difference to customers, employees and the business.

The operating model matters more than the AI model

There's no shortage of discussion about which AI model is best or which platform an organisation should adopt.

Those are important questions, particularly when considering security, integration and cost.

But I'd argue there's another set of questions that deserves just as much attention.

Who owns the business process we're trying to improve?

Where does work get delayed or passed unnecessarily between teams?

What information do employees need to make better decisions?

Which activities can be automated safely, and which still require human judgement?

And perhaps most importantly, how will we know whether we've actually improved anything?

These questions are about the operating model — how people, processes, information and technology work together.

Without considering the operating model, organisations risk adding AI to processes that are already complicated.

The result might be more technology, but not necessarily a better business.

Making business value visible

One of the interesting things about AI is how easy it can be to demonstrate something impressive.

A chatbot answers a question. An AI agent completes a task. A lengthy document is summarised in seconds.

It's easy to see the potential.

But a successful demonstration isn't the same as a successful business outcome.

Imagine a finance team spending several days each month preparing management reports.

AI might help gather information, explain variances and produce initial commentary.

That could save a considerable amount of manual effort.

But the more interesting question is what happens with the time that's been freed up.

Can the finance team spend more time understanding what is driving performance? Can they identify risks earlier? Can they give business leaders better information when decisions need to be made?

The real value may not be the time saved preparing the report. It may be the better decisions that follow.

That's why AI initiatives need clear business measures from the beginning.

For a customer service team, that might mean faster resolution times or fewer repeat enquiries.

For a finance team, it might mean shorter reporting cycles or more time spent on analysis.

For a supply chain team, it could mean improved order accuracy or fewer avoidable delays.

Whatever the measure, the starting point should be the same: understand today's performance and agree on what improvement would look like.

People are still at the centre

When we talk about AI transformation, it's easy to focus on what the technology can do.

But technology is only part of the story.

People need to understand why things are changing, how AI will help them and what their responsibilities will look like.

Managers need confidence that appropriate controls are in place. Leaders need visibility over performance, risk and accountability.

And employees need the opportunity to build new skills.

This becomes particularly important as AI agents move beyond answering questions and begin taking actions within business processes.

If an agent identifies an issue, who reviews it?

If it recommends an action, who decides whether to proceed?

And if something goes wrong, who is responsible for stepping in?

These aren't reasons to slow down innovation. They're practical questions that need answers before organisations can scale AI with confidence.

The organisations that succeed won't necessarily be those with the most AI tools.

They'll be those that help their people work effectively with AI while maintaining clear ownership and accountability.

A practical way to get started

For business leaders wondering where to begin, I'd suggest resisting the temptation to start with a long list of possible AI use cases.

Instead, choose one business process that matters.

It might be customer onboarding, order fulfilment, financial reporting or case management.

Look for a process where delays, manual effort or disconnected information are affecting the outcome.

Then ask four questions.

Four questions to guide your first AI initiative

1What outcome matters?

Define the business improvement you want.

2How does work happen today?

Identify people, systems, delays and decisions.

3What could work better?

Combine AI, automation and human judgement.

4How will we know?

Set a baseline, measures and clear ownership.

1. What business outcome are we trying to improve?

Be specific. Reducing customer resolution time is a clearer objective than simply improving productivity.

2. How does the process work today?

Understand who is involved, which systems they use, where decisions are made and where work gets held up.

3. What could a better way of working look like?

Consider how AI, automation, connected information and human judgement could work together to simplify the process.

4. How will we measure success?

Agree on the measures that matter, establish a starting point and make someone accountable for the outcome.

Start with something manageable, but meaningful enough to demonstrate value.

Learn from the experience, understand what worked and what didn't, and use those lessons to shape the next opportunity.

You don't need to transform the entire organisation in one go.

You need to make a meaningful improvement, prove its value and build from there.

The bigger picture

AI is giving organisations an opportunity to rethink processes and ways of working that may have remained largely unchanged for years.

But the technology itself isn't the transformation.

The transformation happens when information moves more easily across the business, people can make better decisions, customers receive better service and teams spend less time dealing with unnecessary complexity.

That's why I believe the conversation about AI needs to move beyond what the technology can do.

It needs to focus on what the business could do differently.

Because the real measure of AI success isn't how much AI an organisation uses. It's how much better the organisation performs as a result.
